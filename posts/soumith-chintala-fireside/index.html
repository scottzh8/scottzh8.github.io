Customization Is the Default: Notes from Soumith Chintala's Fireside Chat

SGLang Summit 2026 · Fireside chat · Nov 12, 2026

Event: SGLang Summit 2026, Day 1, Thursday Nov 12, 2026, 1:50–2:20 PM, Fort Mason, San Francisco
Speaker: Soumith Chintala, CTO of Thinking Machines Lab (TML) and co-creator of PyTorch
Format: Fireside chat (moderated Q&A)

These are my notes from the session, paraphrased rather than verbatim. Where I’ve added background, I’ve linked the source. Any mistakes in interpreting the talk are mine.

TL;DR

  • Customization will be the default. Soumith’s personal bet: within 2–3 years, most people will use customized models (e.g., LoRA adapters), not one shared model. The market hasn’t caught up yet because most people don’t know customization is useful.
  • Open-weight labs have a feedback problem. Without owning the product and its users, it’s hard to turn user frustration into evals. Distribution is the moat.
  • Harness vs. weights is not either/or. Use harness (memory, tools, retrieval) to get started; use weight-based training for tacit, “how this company thinks” knowledge. They compound.
  • “Rebasing” is the unsolved packaging problem. How do you carry your fine-tuning forward when a new base model ships? The research exists (e.g., on-policy distillation); a simple construct doesn’t yet.
  • Inkling 2 Small shows the “model factory” at work. 68-hour main pretraining phase, 17M+ RL rollouts in 9 days. The point: training should feel like CI/CD, not a heroic all-hands effort.
  • On humans: The goal is a world where people stay useful and can still affect it. Higher human–AI bandwidth may be how we get there.

Who is TML building for?#

Q: PyTorch targeted a specific group of users (researchers). Who does TML target right now?

What Soumith said

A broader audience. Soumith’s view is that customization will be central to how people use AI.

  • His personal take: in 2–3 years, the default will be everyone using customized models, for example via LoRA. The market still has to evolve, because people don’t yet know customization is useful.
  • The engineering question: how do you make training faster for customization?
  • Today’s landscape: Kimi (Moonshot AI), DeepSeek, OpenAI, and Anthropic each mostly serve a single model to everyone, with little per-user customization.
  • The research question: can you build a model that learns more from fewer examples (better sample efficiency)? Few evals measure this today, and TML spent a lot of time on it.

Context

  • TML’s product for this is Tinker, a training API where you write the loop (forward_backward, optim_step, sample, save_state) and TML runs the GPUs. See One Year of Tinker (Oct 2026) and the Tinker docs.
  • Why LoRA is enough for most customization: LoRA Without Regret shows LoRA matches full fine-tuning on small-to-medium post-training datasets and in RL.
  • TML’s mission statement makes the same argument at length: The Future Worth Building Is Human — “Most AI in use today is trained in a handful of places and then frozen.”
  • Public results from Tinker customers: Bridgewater/UIUC’s text-to-SQL model beat frontier models at ~15% of the cost (source).

Why do closed and open models feel different?#

Q: Why does there seem to be a difference between, say, OpenAI and Kimi in practice?

What Soumith said

Soumith’s answer: it’s about the totality of the eval, not a single benchmark.

  • Open-weight labs often don’t control the product. If you don’t own the product, it’s hard to capture user frustration and turn it into evals.
  • Free usage is a learning channel. Example: offering the model for free through coding tools and routers (e.g., OpenCode), where users effectively contribute data and the lab learns from them. If you’re not part of the product’s distribution, learning from users is harder.
  • Distribution is everything. People have to use the model. Massive distribution is extremely valuable, and that puts open-weight labs in a tough spot.

Context

TML is doing this itself. OpenCode currently offers Inkling 2 Small Free, a limited-time free tier where, per OpenCode’s privacy notes, collected data “is retained and may be used to monitor the model’s real-world performance and for model training” (under TML’s free research API tier terms). Several other labs’ free models on OpenCode use the same trade: free access in exchange for a feedback signal.

Harness-based vs. weight-based training#

Q: When should you improve the harness (memory, tool use, retrieval), and when should you train weights?

What Soumith said

The common view is that weight-based training is better. Soumith reframed it: in which cases is it better?

The deciding question: what are you feeding in? Do you want that knowledge to show up in the model’s reasoning and traces, or do you just need a factual fetch?

The employee analogy:

New hire (day 1 / week 1)Veteran (year 1)
What they knowDocs, wikis, tools they can look upHow the company thinks: tacit norms with no written form
AI equivalentHarness: retrieval, memory, toolsWeights: fine-tuning, RL
When to useYou don’t have much context yet; a good starting pointEnterprises, sovereign AI, local markets, dialects

The pushback: models are now good enough that harness changes deliver quick, effective gains. Will harness training beat weight-based training?

Soumith’s response:

  • Harness gains compound with weight gains; they stack.
  • Even with a fine-tuned model, you still want to cite where the original reference came from, which is the harness’s job.
  • Neither replaces the other. It’s weights + harness.
  • The further off-distribution your task is, the bigger the boost from training.
  • It doesn’t have to be a binary decision; the two are becoming more integrated.

Context

TML’s The Future Worth Building Is Human makes a matching point: “If the user’s values and desires only impact the model through a prompt, the user finds that surface properties change while the deeper habits remain.” It also cites Toyota bringing craftsmen back to the line as a tacit-knowledge example, the same idea as the year-1 employee.

What carries over between model generations? “Rebasing”#

Q: If you train a custom model, what carries over when a new base model comes out?

What Soumith said

Soumith called this rebasing: moving your customization onto either a newer version of the same model or a different model entirely.

  • Tinker wants to give people the right construct for rebasing, so they don’t lose their fine-tuning.
  • The research exists: teacher–student methods, on-policy distillation (OPD), and others.
  • The hard part is packaging that research into a clear, simple construct.

Context

  • On-Policy Distillation (TML, Oct 2025): the student samples its own trajectories and a teacher grades every token. On-policy like RL, dense reward like SFT. A Tinker cookbook recipe reproduces it with LoRA.
  • One Year of Tinker states the goal directly: “Your customization recipe can be applied to new open-weight models as they’re released.”

Inkling 2 Small and the “model factory”#

Inkling 2 Small launched on Tinker on Nov 5, 2026. Its open weights went live on Hugging Face under Apache 2.0 a few hours before this talk, on Nov 12.

Specs (from TML’s post): Mixture-of-Experts, 594B total / 17B active parameters, image input, variable effort, evaluated with up to 1M-token trajectories.

From the talk

  • Main pretraining phase: 68 hours (just under 3 days). It was smooth: not an effort where nobody slept.
  • RL: 17M+ rollouts over 9 days.

From TML's post

Main RL runScale
Compute> 4 × 10²³ FLOPs
GPUs2,048
Duration9 days (1,075 steps)
Rollouts17M+

Rollout lengthmedian 43K tokens, p99 1.5M
Rollout durationmedian 8 min, p99 4 hours

One joint RL run replaced the previous generation’s separate reasoning and agentic runs plus multi-teacher distillation. Pretraining ran natively in NVFP4 on GB300 NVL72 racks.

The model factory#

Q: What can the model factory do now that it couldn’t a year ago?

What Soumith said

  • Before, training was a heroic process: rollouts, data, GPUs, parameters, all hand-managed. Scaling up multiplied the work by an order of magnitude.
  • The old cycle: build a model, people burn out for 3 months, then the GPUs sit idle.
  • Now: systems for pretraining → post-training → eval, made systematic and, ideally, model-agnostic.
  • It’s CI/CD for model training.

On the human advantage#

Q: What makes humans special or useful in a world of capable AI?

What Soumith said

  • Soumith’s vision: a world where people still find themselves useful and keep an identity. People want to affect the world.
  • People are unhappy when they feel they can’t affect the world.
  • If we increase the bandwidth between humans and AI, we may find new ways for people to stay useful.
  • Historical parallel: in 1992, it was hard to predict how the internet would change the world. We’re in a similarly uncanny moment now.

Context

TML’s research on Interaction Models targets that bandwidth problem: “a small text box and a long wait” is too narrow a channel for human intent. Their mission post ends: “We are building technology that lets the born and the made walk the road together.”

Takeaways for builders#

  1. Plan for many models, not one. If customization becomes the default, serving shifts from one big model to many adapters on shared bases. Multi-LoRA serving, adapter routing, and per-tenant evals become core infrastructure.
  2. Start with the harness, graduate to weights. Use retrieval and tools until you hit a ceiling on tacit or off-distribution behavior, then train.
  3. Treat your fine-tune as a recipe, not an artifact. Keep your data, reward functions, and evals versioned so you can rebase onto the next base model.
  4. Own a feedback loop. If you don’t control distribution, find another way to turn real user friction into evals.
  5. Industrialize training. The bar is moving from heroic runs to repeatable pipelines.
