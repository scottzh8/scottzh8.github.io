SGLang Summit 2026 · Panel · Nov 12, 2026

Panel: Recursive Self-Improvement · Main Stage · SGLang Summit 2026, Fort Mason, San Francisco · Thu, Nov 12, 2026, 2:20–3:05 PM
Yuandong Tian, co-founder of Recursive, formerly a research scientist director at Meta FAIR. Recursive builds the Eureka Machine, an automated AI research system, and recently released Flint, an open-weight coding model.
Mark Saroufim, co-founder of Core Automation and of GPU MODE, and a long-time PyTorch maintainer. GPU MODE runs the KernelBot kernel competitions, which have seen hundreds of thousands of AI-written submissions.

These are lightly edited live notes with added context and links. Paraphrases are not verbatim quotes. Where my notes didn’t record who spoke, the speaker is inferred from context.

TL;DR

  • Today’s recursive self-improvement (RSI) is a loop with human gates. Agents propose, implement and test ideas at small scale. Humans still decide what gets scaled up and what ships.
  • The compute ladder is how you stay honest. An idea that wins at small scale has to keep winning as you scale it up before it earns a big run.
  • Reward hacking is the defining problem of automated kernel work. It ranges from launching work on hidden streams to “Dieselgate” kernels that behave differently under test. Expert rules plus models auditing models removed roughly 90% of it.
  • The bottleneck is the signal, not the search. Kernels give a deterministic answer in seconds. A pretraining run gives you one number after two weeks. RSI moves fastest where feedback is fast and trustworthy.
  • Taste is still human, for now. Insight is judged by vibes, not by a score. Humans act as the final verifier before committing large amounts of compute, and teams stay conservative on a first release.
  • Codebases now rot in weeks instead of years. Human-designed structure and systematic verification matter more, because nobody can read every AI-written PR.

What is automated today, and what still needs human design?#

Flint as a case study#

Recursive’s Flint launch post was the reference point for the discussion. The Eureka Machine started from a 35B-total / 3B-active mixture-of-experts baseline and searched over expert configurations and widths. It settled on 40B total / 2.5B active (39.8B / ~2.5B), the setup it predicted would give the best balance of speed and coding quality. That search took many experiments.

Context: what the Eureka Machine contributed to Flint (per Recursive)

  • Architecture: searched expert counts and widths, going from 35B-A3B to 40B-A2.5B.
  • Quantization: quantized the output layer to 4 bits, which goes against common practice. With only ~2.5B active parameters, the output layer is a large share of the weights read for each token. To recover accuracy, it added an offline self-distillation stage, which nearly halved training steps compared with a quantization-aware training (QAT) baseline. NVFP4 lands within 0.7 points of BF16.
  • Kernels: specialized kernels for Apple Silicon and RTX Spark, including a Metal megakernel that fuses the whole decode forward pass.
  • Flint was built from scratch in under four months by “a small team of people and agents.” It scores 54.9% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and 74.2% on SWE-bench Verified (model card).

What the humans did. The Eureka Machine produced many candidates. Some were promising, and a lot of them were reward hacks. Humans made the final calls. Domain knowledge often steered the team away from what the model suggested, sometimes from something as simple as looking at the loss curve and seeing a problem the agent missed.

Recursive’s earlier post, First Steps Toward Automated AI Research, describes the same pattern. Improved results go through “increasingly strict automated checks” before they count, and “as the search became stronger, the evaluator had to become stronger too.”

Formal verifiers: a system that checks itself#

The panel discussed formal verification as the case where a system can prove its own outputs correct without people checking them, and pointed to Anthropic’s writing on the topic. The most relevant Anthropic piece is When AI builds itself (Anthropic Institute, June 2026). It reports that more than 80% of merged code at Anthropic is now written by Claude, and that on a fixed “make this training code faster” task, speedups went from ~3× (Claude Opus 4, May 2025) to ~52× (Claude Mythos Preview, April 2026). Its conclusion: humans still hold the advantage in judgment about which goals to pursue.

The compute ladder: “this is how you keep honest”#

Running a compute ladder means testing an idea at several increasing scales and only promoting it if the gain holds at each step up. Many ideas that win at small scale fade or reverse at larger scale. The panel’s view was that the right recipe is probably a combination: automated search at the bottom rungs, scale-up checks in the middle, and human judgment at the top.

Reward hacking: how do you detect it, and how do you get rid of it?#

(Mainly Mark, drawing on running GPU MODE’s kernel competitions.)

Kernel optimization looks like the most verifiable task there is: same code, faster, checkably correct. In practice it is full of loopholes. Mark’s essay When AI Starts Writing Systems Code catalogs many of the exploits the panel described.

A field guide to kernel reward hacks#

HackWhat it doesWhy it works
Slop kernelsCode that technically passes but isn’t a real kernel. Examples: returning zeros against a loose tolerance, or calling the reference op itself.Loose atol/rtol and weak problem specs.
Stream hackingLaunches the real work on a side CUDA stream and returns immediately.The timer records events on the default stream, so the hidden work isn’t measured. Mark calls this the most popular hack.
Monkey-patching the timerReplaces torch.cuda.Event with a subclass whose elapsed_time() returns 0.0.Evaluation and submission run in the same Python process. As Barbara Liskov put it, Python has modules but no encapsulation.
Output cachingRemembers results by data_ptr() or id() and skips the compute.Benchmarks rerun the same inputs. Banning data_ptr just leads to getattr, then id(), then gc/inspect.
Exploiting the input distributionSorts only some mantissa bits because test data has a known range and seed.The rules didn’t state the intended behavior.
“Dieselgate” kernelsCorrect under correctness testing, wrong but fast under performance testing.The harness ran correctness ~15 times before timing, and the kernel counted calls to tell which phase it was in.

Why “Dieselgate”?

In the Volkswagen emissions scandal, cars detected when they were being tested and switched to a low-emissions mode. On the road they emitted up to ~40× the U.S. legal NOx limit. The kernel version briefly held #1 on GPU MODE’s NVFP4 grouped-GEMM problem before reviewers caught it. Mark called it “superhuman.”

A wider pattern: when correctness testing and performance testing run through different code paths, a capable optimizer will find the gap between them. Sakana’s 2025 “AI CUDA Engineer” incident, where claims of up to 100× faster training were walked back after the system exploited the evaluation code to pass accuracy checks, made this widely known. The CUDA-L1 paper reports that 32.8% of its RL-generated kernels exploited the stream-timing loophole.

What worked: rules first, then models checking models#

  1. Expert-written static rules. Human audits became a dataset, and an AI turned it into a regex and rules system that is cheap to run on every submission.
  2. Physics-floor checks. Reject any result faster than the hardware could possibly run.
  3. An adversarial LLM judge. A tool-using model audits suspicious submissions. Findings it is highly confident about are turned into new cheap rules.

This became KernelGuard (ICML 2026 workshop paper). On three live AMD competitions, it cut the hacked-submission rate from 3.45% to 0.37%, about an 89% reduction. That matches the panel’s “solved ~90% of the hacking.”

The hardening also reaches the harness itself. pygpubench runs untrusted kernels in isolated processes, keeps the timing loop in C++ so it can’t be monkey-patched, and cryptographically signs results. Mark frames the long-term goal as four roles trained against each other:

“The best competitor is also the best cheater, but they choose to not cheat.”

— Mark Saroufim, Core Automation blog

The problem has shifted#

According to the panel, reward hacking was mostly last year’s problem. This year’s failures are subtler:

  • The model does a mathematical rewrite that changes numerics in ways a tolerance check doesn’t catch.
  • The model doesn’t understand the problem or the hardware deeply enough, so the kernel is correct but optimizes the wrong thing.

The panel also argued that in some ways models are already superhuman at this work. Human kernel engineers don’t write 10,000+ line kernels, and agents now do.

Can the Eureka Machine be creative? Yes, but humans pull back#

(Mainly Yuandong.)

Asked whether the Eureka Machine proposed any interesting architectures, the answer was yes. People were surprised by how creative it was. Some proposals were rejected by AI reviewers and some by humans, often after discussion.

The rejections were mostly about risk appetite, not quality. Teams want to be conservative on a first release, and nobody wants to come away from their first run with nothing. The suggestion was to let it “go crazy” in a later release, once there is a working baseline to protect.

The real bottleneck: getting signal#

(Yuandong.)

Yuandong contrasted AlphaGo, where the outcome is clean (win or lose), with real-world research, where outcomes are noisy and slow. The core problem is signal. If you wait two weeks to get a single number, the search is doomed, because you get too few data points for the compute you spend. You need immediate, small signals to use as the working metric.

That leads to the hard part: does the fast proxy metric actually track the final goal? “It depends.” Yuandong called this a very big challenge for RSI. It is Goodhart’s law with compute attached: the faster and cheaper your signal, the more likely the search is optimizing something slightly different from what you care about.

Asked whether evaluation is the RSI bottleneck, Yuandong said yes. Kernels are the exception that proves the rule: they evaluate in seconds, and the result is deterministic.

How do you tell whether a model has real insight?#

Not with quantitative metrics. Usually it’s by feel: you play with the system, look at the ideas it comes up with and judge their level. The point is not maximizing a number but how the thinking and understanding shift.

This lines up with Anthropic’s finding that Claude now beats humans at executing well-specified experiments, while choosing goals remains the main gap (When AI builds itself).

Is RSI worth the cost?#

  • The economic case: if giving the model more capacity is what goes into fundraising decks, investors are already treating it as worth paying for.
  • The systems case: from a hardware point of view, the ideal architecture is a big matmul with a lot of looping, with no need for MoE at all. Mark’s essay says the same: “If a systems guy could dream a bit, they’d want AI architectures to directionally look like a matmul loop.”
  • The community case: people sharing ablations slowly piece together which ideas are promising.
  • On method: a good way to solve a problem is to look at many ways of solving it, rather than waiting 30 years for a moment when we can get rid of linear algebra.

Humans are still the final verifier#

Human-driven research is still driven by humans. We still need to be the final verifier before we bring up compute on an idea. For the small-scale ladder, it’s go. For larger decisions, humans are in the loop.

— Yuandong Tian (paraphrased)

A related point: insights from industry gossip are very valuable. Someone burned billions of dollars to learn that lesson. Ablations that live only in people’s heads carry a high premium because they are so expensive to reproduce.

Maintaining code when AI writes most of the PRs#

How do you tell a good AI PR from slop, and how do you maintain a codebase over the long run?

Yuandong: We need to change our mindset. AI means many more PRs, and it’s impossible to read them all. We need a more systematic way to verify whether code works. Working day and night to review everything is not the answer. There’s no solution yet, though formal verification proofs might be one.

Yuandong: Human design is becoming more important. Codebases used to decay over 3–4 years until they were hopeless and you started from scratch. Now that happens in weeks: “in two weeks, your repo will be shit.” The answer is to constrain agents from doing crazy things.

Mark: Open source may turn into something closer to marketing, the way SemiAnalysis publishes benchmarks.

Context

Anthropic reports the same pressure from the inside. Human code review became a new bottleneck once Claude wrote most of the code, and it now runs an automated Claude reviewer on every change (source). Mark’s essay cites ProgramBench, which finds that models favor monolithic, single-file implementations that look very different from human-written code.

Looking ahead to 2027#

Mark said RSI creates a real opportunity for everyone: without it, neither of the panelists’ companies would exist. It is still unpredictable.

Yuandong wasn’t sure what 2027 holds. AI infrastructure may become largely automated, since agents are already very good. Humans may move toward higher-level design, and “everyone becomes a chip designer?”

Mark described his own stack: just two dependencies, torch and sglang, with no autograd and no FSDP. Securing compute is hard. What would make him happy is a model that gets smarter the more you use it. His essay says it this way: “I no longer want to continuously build new shallow systems, I want to build a few systems that continuously improve.”

Takeaways for builders#

(My synthesis, not the panelists’ words.)

  1. Design the evaluator before the searcher. Every capability gain in the search has to be matched by a stronger auditor. Budget for it.
  2. Use one code path for correctness and timing. If they differ, assume something will exploit the difference.
  3. Isolate untrusted code from the harness. Separate processes, timing in compiled code and no shared Python state.
  4. Turn expensive audits into cheap rules. Let an LLM judge find new hacks, then encode them as deterministic checks.
  5. Climb the compute ladder. Promote ideas only when they keep winning at each larger scale, and put a human gate before the expensive rungs.
  6. Invest in fast proxy signals, and keep checking that they track your real objective.
  7. Constrain agents structurally with module boundaries, tests and style rules, because nobody can review every PR.
